Three users check out at the same time. One payment fails. Here is what a line per step
leaves in your log:

```text
checkout started user_id=u_1
checkout started user_id=u_2
checkout started user_id=u_3
cart loaded user_id=u_2 items=3
cart loaded user_id=u_3 items=3
charging card last4=4242
cart loaded user_id=u_1 items=3
payment failed
charging card last4=4242
charging card last4=4242
order created id=o_u_3
order created id=o_u_1
```

Read it again and answer one question: whose payment failed?

Nothing in those twelve lines says. `payment failed` sits between `u_2` and `u_3`, and it is
not adjacent to either for any reason other than scheduling. You can work it out by
elimination, because three checkouts produced two `order created` lines and `u_2` is the one
missing, but that is reasoning from absence. It works here because there are three requests.
It does not work at four hundred.

The lines are not badly written. Every one of them carries a real fact. The problem is that
the facts were separated at birth and the log has no way to put them back together.

## One row per operation

The same three checkouts, logged as wide events:

```json
{"level":"error","service":"checkout","duration_ms":3.27,"op":"checkout","user":{"id":"u_2"},"cart":{"items":3},"payment":{"last4":"4242"},"error":{"message":"Payment failed","status":402,"code":"CARD_DECLINED","why":"Issuer declined the charge","fix":"Try a different payment method","type":"WidelogError"}}
{"level":"info","service":"checkout","duration_ms":6.45,"op":"checkout","user":{"id":"u_3"},"cart":{"items":3},"payment":{"last4":"4242"},"order":{"id":"o_u_3"}}
{"level":"info","service":"checkout","duration_ms":9.87,"op":"checkout","user":{"id":"u_1"},"cart":{"items":3},"payment":{"last4":"4242"},"order":{"id":"o_u_1"}}
```

Twelve lines became three rows, and the question answers itself. `u_2` failed, the issuer
declined it, the cart had three items, it took 3.27ms to find out, and the person reading this
at 3am is told what to try next.

Nothing was stitched together to get there. The fields were written by four different
functions during the request, and they landed in one object because
[`use_logger()`](/reference/api) reads a `contextvars` slot rather than taking a logger
argument. Concurrency is what makes line logs ambiguous, and it is exactly what `contextvars`
is built to keep straight.

<Aside type="note" title="This is the same log volume">
  Three rows instead of twelve lines is not a compression trick. It is the same information
  with the joins already performed, written once at the end of the operation instead of in
  pieces along the way.
</Aside>

## The failure nobody reported

Attribution is the obvious win. This one is easier to miss.

```json
{"level": "warn", "op": "checkout", "retries": 2, "status": 200}
```

That user got a clean 200. Their order went through, they noticed nothing, and they will not
be filing a ticket. But the pricing service reset the connection twice on the way, and the row
says so.

Wide events record the operation, not the failure. Every checkout emits a row whether it
succeeded, failed, or succeeded the third time. That gives you three things without writing
any extra instrumentation:

- a denominator, so an error rate is `errors / operations` rather than `errors / guesswork`
- degraded paths that still returned 200: retries, fallbacks, slow dependencies
- `duration_ms` on every row, so a latency regression shows up without a separate metric

A line-per-step setup records the retry only if somebody thought to log the retry. Here it is
recorded because recording the operation is the whole design.

## Two bugs wearing the same face

Both of these reached the user as `500`, and both say `RuntimeError: checkout failed`:

```text
outer=RuntimeError   root=KeyError       at services/cart.py:35
outer=RuntimeError   root=TimeoutError   at services/gateway.py:35
```

One is a cache miss. One is a payment gateway that stopped answering. They need different
people woken up, and at the surface they are indistinguishable.

`error.causes` is the chain behind the error, following `__cause__` before `__context__`, so
`raise ... from exc` gives you the real origin rather than whatever happened to be in flight.
Because `causes[-1].type` is a field and not a substring of a message, you can group on it,
count it, and alert on it. Alert on the root cause, not on the symptom that fifty different
bugs share.

The `stack` entries point at your code: widelog filters its own frames out before recording
them, so the first line you read is never the logging library's.

## Handing it to a model

Everything above applies equally to a human reading the log and to a model doing it. But two
properties matter more when the reader is a model.

The first is that a model asked to explain the twelve-line version will answer. It will pick a
user, describe the failure confidently, and be wrong some fraction of the time, because
interleaved lines are precisely the shape that invites a plausible guess. Given one row, there
is nothing to guess at.

The second is redaction. Look back at the wide events: `payment.last4` is there and the
idempotency token is not, because keys ending in `token`, `password`, `secret`,
`authorization`, `apikey`, and `cookie` are replaced at any depth before anything is written.
Pasting logs into a model hosted by somebody else is a decision about where your credentials
end up. It is a much easier decision when they were never in the log.

`why` and `fix` help too, for a reason worth stating plainly: you wrote them. A model reading
`CARD_DECLINED` with `why: Issuer declined the charge` is repeating a fact, not forming a
hypothesis.

## What this does not do

widelog produces the evidence. It does not look at it. There is no alerting, no aggregation,
no anomaly detection, and no sampling — those belong to whatever you point `sink` at.

The gap worth knowing about before you build on this: there is no correlation identifier
across services. Within one process the `contextvars` slot keeps a request's fields together,
and on Lambda the X-Ray `trace_id` is picked up automatically, but a failure that spans three
services gives a reader no key to join on unless you set one yourself. If that is your shape,
put a request id in the event at the edge and thread it through, and the rows will line up.
